Let’s be frank. People-pleasing is a special kind of hell – a habit that so many sensitive, empathic, and deep-thinking people fall into. I bet you know the feeling. 

It’s that sickly sweet I’m going to bend over backward to make you feel good no matter what kind of sensation.

And for those reading this thinking “nah, that’s not me, I’ve matured out of that” … yeah, maybe. But people-pleasing can show up in surprising ways. I’ll get to that soon (hint: you may be a “resistor” type).

I have always been something of a people-pleaser. Growing up, I took pride in being the “good girl,” getting good grades, and being helpful. As an adult, I still occasionally overgive, overdo, and worry too much about how others feel, taking responsibility for their feelings.

A couple of days ago I bumped into someone I know unexpectedly at the shops, and it brought up that old pattern again. For me, it’s an automatic habit of masking (something neurodivergent folks know well) or trying to appear “normal” because let’s face it: I’m still a little insecure around people I don’t know well. I still care what others think about me. Maybe less than I did before, but I still do. That’s part of being human. 

After the painful trying-to-appear-normal smile faded from my face in that conversation (which was actually lovely), it got me thinking about people-pleasing. Why does it happen? Why do we do it? What’s the deeper current underneath – the core wound that triggers this behavior? How could this be an inner work opportunity? Because when all is said and done, people-pleasing is a product of unconscious fear, fear that usually has its roots in childhood trauma.

The Four Types of People-Pleasers

In her book Please Yourself: How to Stop People-Pleasing and Transform the Way You Live, psychotherapist Emma Reed Turrell defines the four types of people-pleasers. Pay special attention to which one you identify with the most:

  1. The Classic People-Pleaser: this is the stereotype we usually think about when we hear “people-pleaser.” It’s someone who feels a sense of pride in their ability to endlessly put others first, often basing their self-esteem on how good those around them feel. If others aren’t happy, they interpret it as a personal failure.
  2. The Shadow People-Pleaser: this is the quieter cousin of the classic people-pleaser. It’s someone who carries a fundamental belief that others are simply more important than themselves, and they don’t make a big deal about chronically seeking to please those around them.
  3. The Pacifier People-Pleaser: this is a more self-protective type of behavior. It manifests in people who are terrified of displeasing others and rocking the boat, so they push aside their feelings and placate people instead. 
  4. The Resistor People-Pleaser: this is the type most don’t know about. It appears in those who don’t identify with the people-pleaser label because they don’t show many of the same behaviors. But this is only because they’re so concerned with being judged and displeasing others that they keep everyone at arm’s length to prevent that from happening in the first place. Resistors are generally aloof, avoidant, and overly self-sufficient because deep down, they secretly care a lot about others’ opinions. The logic is that if they opt out, they can’t enter the people-pleasing game to begin with, but this still limits their freedom because they’re living in reaction to a deeper fear.  

Let me know which one you relate to the most. Also know that these aren’t rigid categories: we can and do shift in and out of them depending on the context and people around us.

The Core Wounds Beneath People-Pleasing

The ‘core wound’ is a term sometimes given to describe that tendency we have to believe ourselves unworthy at some deep, hard to define level … Everyone experiences the ‘core wound’ differently.
– Robert A. Johnson

I am shocked by how absent the discussion of core wounding is in the healing field. Perhaps “trauma” comes the closest. But this term doesn’t describe the why behind the pain, only the what.

Core wounding explains why we often think the way we do, why we feel certain disturbing emotions, and the resulting behaviors and habits that come as a result. It is the most unconscious and painful belief we carry about ourselves, others, and the world.

For example, we may have been raised by a narcissistic or emotionally neglectful parent. That creates trauma. But why? Because of core wounding. 

(Incidentally, understanding this topic was a huge unlock in my personal life and healing journey. It helped me to develop self-compassion and nurture my inner child in ways I couldn’t do previously.)

So what are the core wounds related to these four people-pleasing types? To clarify something important: I’m giving you a “flavor” of what the core wound may sound like. Your core wound may be entirely different. This is just a “hint,” not the indisputable truth. This is why finding your own core wound (the precise language that illuminates its true nature) is so important. Words can be walls or windows to healing. More on that later.

Here are the core wounds beneath each type of people-pleasing:

The Classic People-Pleaser = believes that their self-worth is derived from how good they make others feel. “I am worthless.” “I’m not good enough.” 

The Shadow People-Pleaser = believes others are fundamentally more important than they are and lives in the shadows. “I am invisible.” “I am lacking.” 

The Pacifier People-Pleaser = believes that angering or upsetting people directly reflects on their self-worth and value. “I am unsafe.” “I am dislikeable.”

The Resistor People-Pleaser = believes that others are fundamentally unsafe to be around and fears judgment, so isolates and becomes hyper-independent. “I am a failure.” “I am bad.”

Honestly, while I can see myself in many of these people-pleasing types, historically I tend to fall into the Resistor type the most. If I can avoid having to please people, I will – and that means keeping many people at arm’s length. If, for some reason, I do find myself in a situation where I feel obliged to please others, I’ll gravitate toward the Classic mixed with the Pacifier people-pleaser.

What about you? Let me know below.

Naming your core wound is a strange kind of relief. It’s the moment the mental fog clears a little, and you go, oh, so that’s what’s been running the show this whole time. But naming it isn’t the same as healing it. I know that firsthand. I could tell you my core wound in one sentence, and still catch myself acting out the behavior it triggers days later, without noticing until it’s happened.

This is actually part of why I ended up building the Soul Work Compass Course with Sol. It’s not that understanding your wound isn’t valuable. It is, as a starting point. But most stop there. We read the self-help books, we have the “aha” moments, and then… nothing changes. The course exists for those ready to stop avoiding or managing the wound and start living from a different inner place entirely.

This is the Soul-Self whose values, needs, and beliefs are actually your own. That’s what co-creating an aligned and authentic life really means: you go beyond performing it for the approval of others and start building it from the ground up instead. Healing Begins With Security and Clarity

In my experience, people-pleasing starts to lessen when you begin to feel more secure and clear inside of yourself. That security comes through inner work and its four pillars:

  • Embodiment – learning to be more grounded and regulate your nervous system
  • Self-love – learning to be your own best friend and quietening the voice of the inner critic
  • Inner child work – caring for your inner child and learning to reparent them
  • Shadow work – befriending your disowned parts and integrating them into a whole self

Two powerful ways to start I’ve found are: 

(1) Getting grounded by intentionally deepening your breath in situations where you find the people-pleasing arises. This embodiment aspect of inner work also works by looking around your environment for something safe (a plant, tree, painting, cozy spot) to get out of tunnel vision and orient you to the present moment. 

(2) Internally reassuring your inner child that you are safe, lovable, and worthy, especially when you feel the urge to abandon yourself in pursuit of excessively pleasing others.

Those two practices help in the moment. But honestly, moment-to-moment coping was never what actually changed things for me long-term. What made the difference was going deeper and getting clearer. This is not just about “I have a core wound” in the abstract, but knowing precisely what it is, what beliefs it creates, what I actually need instead, and what values I want to be living by rather than the ones fear handed me.

That’s the whole process the Soul Work Compass walks you through. Twelve lessons, a workbook, spiritual alchemy as the map, and at the end you walk away with an actual document: your core wounds, your values, your needs, your rewritten beliefs, all crystallized into one thing you can return to whenever the old pattern creeps back in. Because it will creep back in. That’s just how life is. ;)

“Stress, anxiety and depression are caused when we are living to please others.” – Paulo Coelho

Hidden within people-pleasing tendencies is a morsel of light. It shows that you care for others – sometimes so much that you need to isolate yourself to recover. But that care becomes muddied when it’s tied in with self-worth and core wounding.
